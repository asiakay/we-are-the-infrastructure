# Reasoning Kernel

The reasoning kernel is the common operating logic shared by the book, curriculum, templates, cases, and software.

It is deliberately question-driven. The framework should increase a person's capacity to reason rather than merely hand them conclusions.

## The loop

### 1. OBSERVE — What is happening?
Describe the condition before explaining it.

Capture:
- events;
- experiences;
- patterns;
- changes over time;
- discrepancies;
- immediate consequences.

Separate observation from interpretation wherever possible.

### 2. VERIFY — What do we actually know?
Establish the evidentiary basis of the situation.

Ask:
- What is the claim?
- What evidence supports it?
- What is the source?
- What remains uncertain?
- What would change our confidence?
- Whose knowledge is missing?

The inability to fully describe a situation does not mean there is nothing to know—or nothing to do.

### 3. MAP — What constitutes the system?
Identify the relevant structure.

Map:
- actors;
- institutions;
- relationships;
- assets;
- resources;
- authority;
- incentives;
- information flows;
- money flows;
- constraints;
- historical decisions;
- feedback loops;
- delays.

### 4. DIAGNOSE — What mechanisms produce the condition?
Move from description to causal explanation.

Ask:
- What keeps reproducing this outcome?
- Which mechanisms are necessary or sufficient?
- What is structural and what is contingent?
- What are we assuming?
- What competing explanations fit the evidence?

A diagnosis should remain revisable.

### 5. LOCATE LEVERAGE — What can we change?
Distinguish between what matters and what can presently be influenced.

Ask:
- Where could an intervention alter system behavior?
- Who possesses relevant authority or resources?
- What existing assets are underused?
- What is within our current sphere of agency?
- What could become influenceable if another capability existed first?

### 6. SEQUENCE — What happens first?
Strategy is temporal.

Ask:
- What are we trying to change?
- By when?
- In what sequence?
- What dependencies exist?
- What must be true before the next move becomes viable?
- Which actions preserve future options?

The best first action is not necessarily the most visible action. It is often the action that changes the feasible set for everything that follows.

### 7. BUILD CAPABILITY — What must we become able to do?
Identify the missing function between intention and execution.

Ask:
- What capability does this strategy require?
- Do we already possess it?
- At what level: individual, relational, network, community, or institutional?
- Can it be learned, acquired, partnered for, or built?
- How will it persist?

### 8. ACT — What bounded intervention will test the strategy?
Translate reasoning into a concrete intervention.

Define:
- actor;
- action;
- target;
- timing;
- resources;
- expected result;
- risk;
- evidence to collect.

Prefer actions that generate useful information even when the desired outcome is not achieved.

### 9. REFLECT — What happened?
Treat outcomes as evidence.

Ask:
- What changed?
- What did not change?
- What surprised us?
- Which assumptions survived?
- Which assumptions failed?
- What capability did we gain?
- What did the action cost?
- What should be preserved as institutional memory?

### 10. EXPAND POSSIBILITY — What does that change make possible?
This is the compounding step.

Ask:
- What is possible now that was not possible before?
- What new leverage exists?
- What new relationships, assets, knowledge, legitimacy, resources, or capabilities exist?
- What is the next strategic question?

Then re-enter the loop.

## Kernel shorthand

**Observe → Verify → Map → Diagnose → Leverage → Sequence → Build → Act → Reflect → Expand**

## Two governing questions

Across the entire kernel, two questions function as the strategic hinge:

> **What can we change?**

and then:

> **What does that change make possible?**

The first locates agency in the present.

The second projects agency through time.

## Design implication for software

The application should not merely store answers to these questions. It should help users move through them, preserve evidence and reasoning, expose relationships among objects, revisit assumptions, and carry learning from one cycle into the next.

The software's job is to make disciplined strategic practice easier to repeat—not to replace human judgment.
