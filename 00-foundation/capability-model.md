# Capability Model

## Core definition

A **capability** is the reliable capacity of a person, network, community, or institution to produce or influence an outcome under real conditions.

Information is not automatically capability.

Knowing *about* a city budget is different from being able to read one, identify a consequential allocation, determine who controls it, organize relevant people, intervene at the appropriate decision point, and evaluate whether the intervention changed anything.

WE ARE THE INFRASTRUCTURE therefore treats capability—not content consumption—as the primary unit of development.

## Capability ladder

### 1. Individual capability
A person can reliably perform an action or reasoning process.

Examples:
- verify a public claim;
- analyze a budget;
- map a decision process;
- articulate a strategic objective.

### 2. Relational capability
People can find one another, establish trust, exchange information, and coordinate complementary skills or resources.

### 3. Network capability
A group can coordinate distributed people and resources toward a shared outcome without depending entirely on one central actor.

### 4. Community capability
A community can repeatedly perform a function it needs: preserve knowledge, distribute resources, educate members, create economic activity, respond to emergencies, influence decisions, or produce media.

### 5. Institutional capability
The function becomes sufficiently formalized, documented, resourced, governed, and transmissible that it can persist through turnover and changing conditions.

## Capability formation

The developmental pathway is:

**Formation → Practice → Vocabulary → Formalization → Synthesis → Transmission → Institutionalization**

### Formation
What has lived experience, culture, family, community, work, conflict, creativity, or survival already taught us?

### Practice
What have we learned through actually doing things?

### Vocabulary
What concepts allow us to name patterns we may already recognize?

### Formalization
Can tacit knowledge be made explicit enough to examine, improve, repeat, and teach?

### Synthesis
How does this knowledge connect with evidence, history, other disciplines, and other people's experience?

### Transmission
Can another person develop the capability without reproducing the originator's biography?

### Institutionalization
Can the capability survive personnel changes, preserve memory, maintain standards, and adapt to new conditions?

## Capability object

Every capability tracked by the framework should eventually be describable through a common schema:

- **Name** — what capability is this?
- **Outcome** — what does it make possible?
- **Level** — individual, relational, network, community, institutional.
- **Prerequisites** — what must already exist?
- **Inputs** — information, people, time, money, tools, authority, relationships.
- **Practice** — what must someone repeatedly do?
- **Evidence of competence** — how do we know the capability exists?
- **Constraints** — under what conditions does it fail?
- **Dependencies** — what other capabilities does it rely on?
- **Transmission method** — how can others acquire it?
- **Persistence mechanism** — how does it survive turnover?
- **Next possibilities** — what becomes feasible once it exists?

## Capability gap

A **capability gap** exists when an intended change is plausible but the actors involved cannot yet reliably perform one or more functions required to produce it.

This distinction matters because many apparent motivation, participation, or resource problems are actually capability problems.

The framework should therefore ask, before prescribing more activity:

> What would we need to be able to do reliably for this strategy to work?

## Compounding capability

Capabilities can alter the feasible set of future actions.

A strategic act therefore has two outputs:

1. the immediate outcome it produces; and
2. the new capability or possibility it creates.

This yields the recurring question:

> **What does that change make possible?**

The desired trajectory is not a circle returning to the same point. It is an upward spiral in which learning, resources, relationships, and institutional memory increase the capacity for subsequent action.

## Persistence test

Before considering a capability mature, ask:

> If the person currently carrying this function disappeared tomorrow, what would remain?

Documentation alone is insufficient. Durable capability may require trained people, shared access, governance, relationships, resources, routines, data, tools, and succession mechanisms.

**The capability might outlive you. This is a design requirement, not an accidental benefit.**
